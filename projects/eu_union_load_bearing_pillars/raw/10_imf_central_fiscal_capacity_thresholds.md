Title: A Central Fiscal Stabilization Capacity for the Euro Area
URL: https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/Staff-Discussion-Notes/Issues/2018/03/22/A-Central-Fiscal-Stabilization-Capacity-for-the-Euro-Area-45741
Date: 2018-03-26

Claim / relevance:
- This source gives an externally grounded calibration for what a euro-area central fiscal capacity would need to look like to count as materially stabilizing rather than merely symbolic.
- It is directly relevant to the thesis's unresolved threshold for "material" central fiscal capacity.

Key facts / excerpts:
- The IMF note argues that a euro-area central fiscal capacity should be large enough to provide meaningful macroeconomic stabilization, not just exist institutionally.
- In the IMF baseline calibration, each country contributes 0.35% of GDP annually to a central fund, with transfers of 0.5% of GDP for every 1 percentage point unemployment deviation above trend.
- Under that calibration, the fund would have accumulated about 1.6% of euro-area GDP in assets before the global financial crisis, with aggregate annual gross transfers peaking around 1.1% of euro-area GDP.
- The paper explicitly treats 0.1% of GDP annual contributions as too small because that would force the mechanism to borrow continually, while contributions above 0.5% of GDP per year would lead to excessive asset accumulation.
- The note also states that only about 20% of idiosyncratic shocks are smoothed across euro-area countries, compared with roughly three-quarters across US states, reinforcing the claim that existing euro-area cross-border stabilization is thin relative to a mature federation.

Why this matters for the thesis:
- This is the strongest threshold-grounding source so far because it gives a concrete range for a non-trivial euro-area stabilization capacity instead of leaving "material" undefined.
- It supports an inference that a durable-equilibrium-grade fiscal pillar would need a standing capacity on the order of low-single-digit percentages of GDP in assets and around 1% of GDP peak transfer capability, not just temporary or discretionary crisis instruments.
- It also helps distinguish a real standing stabilizer from improvised crisis backstops: the IMF design is rules-based, automatic, and pre-funded, which matches the thesis's Mode DE direction more closely than the EU's current discretionary architecture.
