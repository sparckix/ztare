Agent demos make agents act. Real organizations make them accountable.

A chat agent works fine while you are watching it. The trouble starts when you stop watching: it spent more than expected, it edited the wrong file, it told you the job was done when it was not. By then there is no record of who said yes.

The chat-agent default

  • You type, it acts. Memory is the chat scrollback.
  • Permission is whatever the underlying tool happens to allow.
  • Approvals are pop-ups. Once dismissed they are gone.
  • Two agents working together share nothing but text.
  • "What did the agent do last Tuesday?" is unanswerable.

An organization runtime

  • Each role has explicit limits — what it can spend, what files it can touch, what it must escalate.
  • Approvals queue up like an inbox. They survive restarts and are visible to anyone you give access to.
  • Agents can hand work to each other through durable, typed messages — not by guessing in chat.
  • Every decision the org makes is recorded as one line in a tamper-evident log you can replay later.
  • You can drive the whole thing from a dashboard, your phone, or a plain folder of files — they all show the same thing.

What you get out of the box.

Six building blocks. They work for any kind of work — research, operations, legal review, customer support, anything where you need agents to act with boundaries.

1Roles with limits

Every agent has a written job description, a daily spend cap, a list of files it may touch, a list it cannot, and a list of decisions it must escalate. Tested against a contract before any agent boots, so a misconfigured role cannot ship work.

2Mandates in plain language

The contract says what an agent can do; the mandate says what it must do — written in prose, version-controlled. When the contract and the mandate disagree, a published rule decides which wins. No surprises.

3An approval queue

One inbox of pending approvals. Each card carries a subject, choices, a deadline, what happens if you do nothing, and who else needs to sign. Resolve from anywhere; the answer lands as a record you can audit later.

4Internal mail

Agents can ask each other for help, hand off a piece of work, request clarification, or post a status — through durable typed envelopes, not lost chat messages. A request creates an obligation; an obligation gets resolved.

5Deadline pressure

Tasks carry deadlines and budget caps. The system raises pressure on items that are running long or running hot, and surfaces a warning before something silently overruns. No more "we forgot about that one."

6A replayable audit trail

Every decision the org makes — every approval, directive, and control action — lands as one timestamped row in an append-only log. Walk it forward, walk it back, hand it to an auditor. Designed to add tamper-evidence as you scale.

Three ways to drive it. One source of truth.

Pick whichever surface fits the moment. They are all reading and writing the same underlying records, so a decision you make in one shows up everywhere within seconds.

The records are the system.

Approvals, role contracts, mandates, internal messages, and the decision log all live as plain files you can read, version, and back up. The dashboard and the phone app are projections — if they break, your data is intact.

Dashboard

A browser view of pending approvals with free-text reasons, an OKR tree, and a control panel for sending instructions and pausing or resuming any role. Use this when you want to think through a decision.

Phone

Push notifications when something needs you. Tap-to-approve, quick digests of what is pending, urgent items only. Locked to your account so nobody else can drive your org. Use this when you are not at the desk.

Files

The plain folder underneath. Good for automation, scripts, and backup. Anything you can do with a button you can do with a file edit, which means the system never has a "you must use the app" moment.

Make it yours in five steps.

You do not write code to adopt this. You edit role descriptions, set your preferences, and the same runtime drives your org — research, operations, legal, growth, anything.

step 1Clone & check Pull the repo and run the included verification script. It tells you in five seconds whether your machine is set up correctly. No agents started yet.
step 2Define your roles Replace the example roles with yours: a researcher, a compliance officer, an operations lead, whatever your team needs. Each role gets a written boundary and a spend cap.
step 3Set your priorities Tell the system what you care about: which work matters most, what counts as urgent, where you want to be the one deciding versus where you want the agents to act on their own.
step 4Turn on the rails One command starts the agents and the phone bot; one starts the dashboard. The setup guide shows the exact commands and what to expect.
step 5Hand it your first task Drop a task into the system. An agent picks it up, works on it, and asks you when it needs human authority. From there it is just running an organization.

Why does this exist?

Because the last century spent fifty years inventing the accountability infrastructure for human organizations — roles, ledgers, charters, sign-offs, audit trails — and AI-native organizations should not have to reinvent every piece of it from scratch. This is a head start.

Built from things that went wrong.

Every primitive here exists because something failed without it: an approval that nobody remembered making, an agent that quietly ran past its budget, a decision log nobody could explain. The building blocks are the ones we wished we had.

Plain files, by design.

The records are on disk. You can read them, search them, back them up, and recover them without restoring a database. The machinery only gets heavier when your scale forces it to — never before.

Works for any kind of work.

The system does not assume what your team does. Scientific research is one example; your operations, legal, growth, or customer-support team is another. You change the role descriptions, not the underlying machinery, and the same governance applies.
