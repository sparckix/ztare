# When a Consciousness Verdict Cannot Be Earned

**A Descent-Theoretic Admissibility Criterion**

A claim that a system is or is not conscious is, in the end, a claim about evidence: it is only as good as the evidence's power to tell consciousness apart from its absence. This manuscript treats consciousness attribution as an identification problem — a verdict is licensed only when the target property can be recovered from a declared observation site, and where it cannot, more data does not help. For a structurally specified class of hidden-variable systems the recovery fails *necessarily*, surviving every admissible revision of the measurement language. The manuscript makes this precise as a single criterion, *revision-stable non-effective descent*, proves the underlying factorization for the finite deterministic and exact-stochastic cases (with eight components machine-checked in Lean 4), and isolates the general reduction as an explicit open obligation. The criterion's normative force is confined to fail-closed audit settings.
