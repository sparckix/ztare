\subtitle{Autonomous, general-purpose formal verification for the
mechanisms that run markets and institutions, and the library it
produces.}
\author{Daniel Alami}
\date{July 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
A machine-checked proof used to cost a specialist six months, and the
specialists were mathematicians with every reason to formalize
perfectoid spaces and none to formalize a credit-agreement covenant. So
the covenant went unformalized, along with the waterfall, the quorum
bound, the pricing invariant, and most of the logic that runs money and
institutions. The people who could verify and the systems worth
verifying were rarely the same.

This paper argues that a closed-loop, general-purpose verification
apparatus collapses that cost toward the cost of compute, and it shows
what falls out. A human writes a natural-language blueprint. From there
the apparatus runs without a verification engineer in the loop: it
formalizes the blueprint in Lean 4 through a faithfulness firewall,
searches for a proof, and re-verifies every closure with a governance
kernel the proving agent cannot influence. Autonomy starts at the
blueprint, where the residual human judgment lives. The output is
\texttt{nonmathlib4}: a growing library of kernel-checked theorems in
finance, strategy, distributed systems, cryptography, compliance, social
choice, and corporate governance, each self-contained, each reporting
only the three standard axioms, none carrying a \texttt{sorry}. We table
nineteen of them and walk through one in full: no round-trip arbitrage
is possible at any state a constant-product market maker can reach.

The kernel certifies that the proof follows from the stated
specification. It says nothing about whether the specification denotes
the concept a human had in mind. Our Shamir secret-sharing result sits
on that line: it proves the algebraic secrecy bijection, not the
measure-theoretic Shannon statement. Owning that boundary is what
separates a verified claim from an oversold one.
\end{abstract}

\setstretch{1.05}
\subsection{1. The verification gap}\label{1-the-verification-gap}

You trust most of the systems you rely on for the same reason: someone
checked, and you take their word. The tests passed. The auditor signed
off. The lawyers initialed every page of the credit agreement. Call it
trust by vouching, and it carries nearly all software, contracts, and
financial machinery. It is also how they fail quietly, when the check
missed a case no one thought to try.

A second kind of assurance is stronger, and you have almost never seen
it applied to a covenant or a settlement rule. A proof assistant like
Lean 4 (de Moura and Ullrich) checks every step of an argument against a
small trusted kernel; a statement it accepts is as certain as anything
in engineering gets, and it leaves a proof a skeptic can re-run. The
reason it stayed inside mathematics and safety-critical hardware is
cost. Producing the proof is hard, and the people who can do it are few.

The community that built Mathlib, Lean\textquotesingle s library of
mathematics (mathlib Community), is mathematicians; they formalized the
definition of a scheme and the theory of perfectoid spaces (Buzzard,
Commelin, and Massot). The lawyers and bankers who work with waterfalls
and covenants do not write Lean. On one side sit the people who can
verify. On the other sit the systems worth verifying. They rarely meet.
The seL4 microkernel, a few thousand lines of operating-system code,
took a team on the order of twenty person-years to prove correct (Klein
and colleagues), which is why almost nothing outside safety-critical
systems ever got the treatment.

That price is what changes now. When the marginal cost of a proof falls
from months of a specialist\textquotesingle s time to a few dollars of
model API calls, the calculus flips. Verifying a covenant stops being a
capital project and becomes something you run the way you run a linter
on a pull request. The question is no longer "can we afford to verify
this?" but "why wouldn\textquotesingle t we?"

Two conditions make the fall real, and both have to hold. The loop has
to run on its own once a human writes the blueprint, with no
formal-methods engineer steering the formalization, the search, or the
check. And the prover has to be general, one system that proves a
market-maker invariant, a quorum bound, and a tax rule rather than a
bespoke tool for each. An autonomous prover locked to one domain solves
a narrow problem; a general prover that still needs an expert to drive
it has not moved the cost. Only both together do.

Michael Porter drew a distinction that fits. Operational effectiveness
is doing the same activities better than rivals; strategic positioning
is doing different activities, or the same ones differently, in a way
rivals cannot easily copy. A faster prover is operational effectiveness,
and it erodes with the next model release. The position that holds is
the machinery that makes an untrusted model\textquotesingle s output
trustworthy --- a different activity, and one that gets better as the
models do instead of eroding.

\subsection{2. The unoccupied
combination}\label{2-the-unoccupied-combination}

Formal verification of business, financial, and legal logic is not new.
Several of these systems run in production, and one built a business.

Imandra, from Aesthetic Integration, is a commercial theorem prover for
financial algorithms. It reasons about trading systems and venue logic
for regulatory compliance under regimes like MiFID II, and it shipped
the first formal verification platform aimed at smart contracts. AWS
Zelkova encodes the semantics of IAM and S3 access policies into SMT and
answers questions like "can any principal outside this account read this
bucket?" It runs millions of times a day, and its companion Tiros
reasons about network reachability. Catala, from Inria, is a language
for writing tax and benefits law as executable, verifiable code, and the
French tax administration uses it. For smart contracts, the Certora
Prover checks Solidity and EVM bytecode against specifications written
in its own CVL language and runs inside production audit workflows,
while KEVM, from Runtime Verification, gives the EVM a complete formal
semantics in the K framework. And the systems-verification tradition,
seL4\textquotesingle s machine-checked microkernel and the DeepSpec line
of verified software, is the proof that whole real artifacts can be
certified end to end.

So the frontier is occupied. What is not occupied is the combination
this apparatus targets. Every system above is either domain-specific,
meaning it verifies one class of thing with a tool built for that class,
or expert-driven, meaning a trained verification engineer writes the
specification and steers the proof. Zelkova verifies access policies and
nothing else. Certora needs a security engineer to write CVL. Catala
needs someone fluent in Catala. The apparatus here is autonomous after
the blueprint, no verification engineer in the loop, and
general-purpose, plain Lean 4 with Mathlib rather than a domain solver.
That pairing lets one system produce an AMM theorem, a bankruptcy
waterfall, a Byzantine bound, and a secret-sharing guarantee from
natural-language blueprints alone.

Every prior system is domain-specific, or expert-driven, or restricted
to trusted benchmarks where the statement is known true, or confined to
static definitions. The combination in the last row, a general-purpose
prover plus post-blueprint autonomy plus a governance loop that
re-checks every closure plus explicit handling of the faithfulness
boundary, is the position none of the others occupy.

The apparatus also draws on a second literature, on autonomous proving
and autoformalization. Autoformalization, translating natural-language
mathematics into a formal system, was shown practical with large
language models by Wu, Jiang, Li, Rabe, Staats, Jamnik, and Szegedy, who
translated a quarter of competition problems perfectly into Isabelle.
Draft, Sketch, and Prove, from Jiang and colleagues, used an informal
proof to guide a formal prover and roughly doubled the success rate on
the miniF2F benchmark. LeanDojo, from Yang and colleagues, built the
retrieval-augmented proving stack for Lean that later work stands on.
Thor and Magnushammer, from Jiang, Mikuła, and their coauthors, wired
language models to premise-selection hammers. HyperTree Proof Search,
from Lample and colleagues, brought AlphaZero-style search and expert
iteration to theorem proving, and DeepMind\textquotesingle s AlphaProof
carried that line to a silver-medal score at the 2024 International
Mathematical Olympiad, training on eighty million autoformalized
statements.

Prior autoformalization and autonomous-proving work optimizes closure
rate on trusted benchmarks, where a competition problem is known to be
true and a compiling proof is the whole game. This apparatus operates in
the untrusted regime, where a compiling proof is necessary but not
sufficient, because the statement was written by the same fallible model
that wrote the proof. There a proof search alone is not enough: the Lean
compiler grounds the model adversarially, forcing it to discard
hallucinations; a governance kernel re-checks every closure; and a
firewall gates every statement before a proof is ever attempted. The
model proposes and the kernel disposes.

Two 2026 systems share parts of the approach. LeanMarathon formalizes
research mathematics autonomously and audits its own statements: a
reviewer agent checks each Lean type against the source paper, and a
build gate keeps the natural-language and formal dependency graphs from
drifting apart. It works from rigorous papers, where the target
statement is already precise, so the task is to verify a faithful
translation, not to decide what the statement should be.
Coelho\textquotesingle s mathematical-finance library carries the same
two trust signals reported here, faithfulness classification and an
axiom-pinning gate, but it is hand-written and stays in the pricing and
stochastic-calculus core. The regime this apparatus is built for is the
one neither enters: a non-mathematical intent, stated in prose by
someone who does not write Lean, where the formal statement can be a
plausible-but-wrong rendering of a covenant or a policy and no rigorous
source exists to check it against. When the model writes both the
statement and the proof, a clean proof of the wrong statement is the
failure mode, and the load-bearing layer is the governance kernel that
re-checks every closure against a statement the model itself produced.

This is a library for the mechanisms markets and institutions run on,
the dynamic multi-step objects rather than a grab-bag of financial
lemmas. An automated market maker is a state machine you trade against.
A waterfall is a distribution procedure. A quorum system is a
fault-tolerance protocol. These are mechanisms with reachable states and
adversarial histories, not static facts.

That framing also fixes the relationship to the one adjacent library.
EconCSLib, from Bei, Ma, Jing, Fu, and Tang, is an early, AI-assisted
Lean 4 library for computational economics. EconCSLib supplies reusable
interfaces and definitions for players, preferences, utilities,
allocations, and mechanisms, the vocabulary a domain needs before it can
state a theorem. This work proves the dynamic, multi-step mechanisms end
to end and autonomously: the AMM that survives any trade history, the
waterfall that stays feasible, the quorum that intersects. The two are
complements, and a mature version of the field wants the shared
vocabulary and the proved mechanisms both, with their interfaces
meeting.

\subsection{3. The apparatus}\label{3-the-apparatus}

We built the apparatus in four stages: formalize, solve, govern,
self-learn. One design rule runs through all four: put determinism
exactly where soundness requires it, at the trust boundary, and give the
agent full freedom everywhere upstream. Too much determinism and you
hand-wire the prover into uselessness. Too little and the agent becomes
its own judge, which is how a wrong proof gets waved through. Knowing
which knob is which is the trick, and the whole architecture is our
answer to it. Throughout, "we" refers to the authors who built and
configured the apparatus; "the apparatus" refers to the running system,
which operates without human intervention once the blueprint is written.

\textbf{Formalize.} The input is a blueprint, a natural-language
description of the theorem and the vocabulary it needs. The apparatus
renders it into a Lean statement. Then, before any proof is attempted, a
faithfulness firewall decides whether that statement means what the
blueprint said. The firewall runs a stack of checks. The statement has
to compile. It has to be non-trivial, so a cheap tactic cannot close it
and it is not vacuously true. Its structure has to match the reference,
meaning the binder counts, the conclusion\textquotesingle s operator,
and the quantifier order line up, which is how a dropped hypothesis or a
weakened conclusion gets caught. For decidable policy fragments the
firewall goes further and returns a typed certificate: either the two
formulations are provably equivalent over the whole domain, or here is a
concrete input that tells them apart, or the question sits outside the
decidable fragment and is declared as such rather than silently waved
through. Last, the statement is back-translated to natural language and
a separate judge has to rule it the same problem. Only then does a proof
get attempted.

Most of these checks are deterministic: the compile check, the
non-triviality check, the structural comparison, and the
decidable-fragment decision procedure run in Lean, a small amount of
Python, and for policy fragments a z3 or Gröbner routine, none of which
the proving model can influence. Only the back-translation check is
itself a language model, and it is drawn from a different model family
than the one that wrote the statement, so a single model cannot both
introduce an error and clear it. The governance stage described below
uses no model at all.

The firewall guarantees fidelity, not truth. Every check answers one
question: does the Lean statement mean the same thing as the blueprint?
None of them answers the prior question of whether the blueprint itself
is a correct statement of the intended result. That question is
undecidable in general and belongs to the human.

\textbf{Solve.} A proof search runs over the formal statement. The
engine is agentic: a frontier model, equipped with tools and a warm Lean
session, decides at each step whether to prove the goal directly,
decompose it into sub-lemmas, or, if it judges the goal false, prove its
negation. When it decomposes, the kernel audits the decomposition,
checking that it is \texttt{sorry}-free, non-circular, uses every lemma,
and actually proves the parent, before the sub-lemmas are attacked in
turn. A pool of diverse proposers can attack a goal in parallel, but
only one serial verifier commits, first valid proof wins. The search has
the usual apparatus, best-first frontier scoring, restarts, value
backup, but none of it touches soundness. It only decides what to try
next.

\textbf{Govern.} This is the boundary, and it is the part a stronger
model cannot give you, because the model is the thing being policed. A
\texttt{closed} verdict from the solver is a proposal, nothing more. It
becomes a result only after a fixed stack of deterministic checks the
agent cannot influence:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The Lean kernel re-verifies the proof term.
\item
  \texttt{\#print\ axioms} on the closed theorem must report a subset of
  \texttt{\{propext,\ Classical.choice,\ Quot.sound\}}. A \texttt{sorry}
  leaves \texttt{sorryAx} in that list and is rejected.
  \texttt{native\_decide} leaves \texttt{Lean.ofReduceBool} and is
  rejected. A smuggled custom axiom is rejected.
\item
  The proved statement, and every definition it rests on, must be
  unaltered: no definition swapped for a shell, no notation or instance
  that quietly changes what the symbols mean.
\item
  The proof must need the blueprint\textquotesingle s own vocabulary. A
  proof that compiles against bare Mathlib was a library lookup, not a
  closure, and the matched-negative-control catches it.
\item
  The statement must not be vacuously true.
\end{itemize}

An adversarial red-team confirms the boundary holds. Every attempt to
smuggle an unsound proof past it, a bare \texttt{sorry}, a nested
\texttt{sorry}, a \texttt{native\_decide} reduction, a false custom
axiom cited as if proven, was rejected, and the clean control passed:
five out of five caught, zero false flags. On an open target where the
model produced three proofs that compiled with clean axioms and no
\texttt{sorry}, the statement-integrity check rejected all three,
because each had faithlessly altered the statement it claimed to prove.
A verifier that trusted "it compiled" would have accepted all three.

\textbf{Self-learn.} Every kernel-closed theorem is banked into a
library the next proof can cite, and this is how the library compounds.
The effect is a change in complexity, not only in convenience: banking a
lemma converts a combinatorial re-search into a linear composition. A
later result does not re-derive the constant-product bound; it cites the
banked lemma and proves only the new content on top. This is the
mechanism behind the numbers in Table 2, where the constant-product
invariant took about five hours to establish and the
no-round-trip-arbitrage result that stands on it closed in under nine
minutes. The library is the environment the engine proves against, and
it grows.

One property of the boundary makes agent-generated proofs safe to trust.
Since soundness lives entirely at the governance stage, everything
upstream is free. A false sub-lemma the agent invents simply fails to
close. A bad decomposition dead-ends. The worst an aggressive,
hallucinating agent can do is leave a recorded gap, never a false
theorem. So we can let a frontier model run wild in the search and still
stand behind every closure.

The cost is worth stating plainly. The AMM temporal result is the
expensive one: about 5.1 hours of wall time, 40 of 104 attempts closing,

it should not read as one. The hardware is a single four-core AMD EPYC
virtual machine with 15 GB of RAM and no GPU. Proof search dispatches

makes, not a cluster reservation. The companion no-cyclic result closed

established theory costs minutes.

\subsubsection{4.1 A worked walkthrough of the AMM no-arbitrage
result}\label{41-a-worked-walkthrough-of-the-amm-no-arbitrage-result}

The AMM no-cyclic theorem is the most business-relevant result in the
library, and it shows the apparatus and the compounding in a single
object.

\textbf{The setting.} A constant-product automated market maker holds
two reserves, \texttt{x} and \texttt{y}, and quotes trades that keep the
product \texttt{x·y} at least constant. It charges a fee \texttt{γ}, a
number in \texttt{(0,1{]}}, where \texttt{γ=1} is a feeless pool and
\texttt{γ\textless{}1} is a pool that keeps a real cut. Traders swap one
token for the other. A round-trip is the natural attack: swap a token
out, then immediately swap the proceeds back, hoping to end with more
than you started. If that ever pays, the pool has an arbitrage, and
someone will drain it.

\textbf{The blueprint.} The natural-language input asks for one thing.
Over a pool with strictly positive reserves and a fee
\texttt{γ∈(0,1{]}}, prove that no trade history enables round-trip
arbitrage. For every well-formed starting pool, every finite prior
sequence of trades in any direction, a flash loan, a sandwich, a cyclic
path, and every subsequent round-trip in either direction, the
round-trip returns at most the input, and strictly less whenever the fee
is real and the input is positive. Equivalently: at no reachable state
can a trader close a loop against the pool for a strictly positive
risk-free return. The blueprint spells out that this must cite the
already-banked constant-product theory rather than re-derive it.

\textbf{The statement.} That blueprint becomes a Lean theorem,
\texttt{no\_history\_enables\_round\_trip\_arbitrage}, a conjunction of
two parts. The first is universal: for every fee, every pool, every
finite trade list, and every subsequent round-trip, the return is at
most the input, and strictly less under a real fee with positive input.
The second is an existence witness that keeps the claim non-vacuous: a
concrete pool, fee, and trade where the strict inequality actually
bites, so the theorem is not true merely because its hypotheses are
never met. The firewall gates this statement, and it clears, because it
means what the blueprint said.

\textbf{What "reachable" buys you.} The earlier result proved the
round-trip loses at a \emph{well-formed} pool. This one proves it loses
at every pool the trader can actually face, which is the earlier pool
after some arbitrary prior history. The bridge is a banked lemma,
\texttt{executeTrades\_keep\_wellFormed}: executing any finite trade
list preserves well-formed reserves throughout. So every reachable pool
is well-formed, the banked round-trip bound applies to it verbatim, and
the proof is a one-step composition. Instantiate the banked bound at the
reachable pool, discharge its well-formedness hypothesis with the banked
invariant, cover both directions and the strict real-fee case. The new
content is the quantification over histories. Everything else is cited.

\textbf{Why it compounds.} The library behaves as an environment rather
than a pile. The no-cyclic proof reuses the round-trip-\texttt{≤}-input
lemmas, the strict real-fee lemmas, and the sequence invariant, all
kernel-clean rungs from the constant-product campaign. It re-derives
none of them. So it closed in minutes where the foundation took hours,
and the marginal cost of the next verified property keeps falling as the
library grows.

\textbf{The receipt.} The closed theorem reports
\texttt{\#print\ axioms} =
\texttt{\{propext,\ Classical.choice,\ Quot.sound\}}, carries no
\texttt{sorry}, and elaborates standalone against Mathlib. Anyone with
the toolchain can run
\texttt{lake\ env\ lean\ amm\_no\_cyclic\_arbitrage.lean} and watch the
kernel accept it. The guarantee is not "a strong model said so." It is a
proof a small trusted kernel checked, re-runnable by a skeptic.

\subsubsection{4.2 Modular constraints}\label{42-modular-constraints}

Constraints are where a clean theorem meets a real system, and a library
of only pristine baselines does not say which constraint breaks which
result. The design answer is modularity: the baseline theorem and the
constrained variant are separate, importable objects, so a practitioner
can take the pure result and test which constraint their system actually
faces breaks it. The pattern is already visible in the filed work. The
pure constant-product no-arbitrage bound is one theorem; the "any
adversarial history, any reachable state" version is another that
imports it. The Byzantine bound is proved for a general adversary of
size \texttt{f} and then instantiated at the tight \texttt{n=3f+1}
witness. The natural continuations follow the same shape: baseline VCG
plus a budget cap, a clean settlement plus integer truncation, an
honest-majority protocol facing Byzantine actors. The library should
make it cheap to import the baseline and prove what survives the
constraint, and costly to conflate the two.

\subsection{5. The faithfulness
boundary}\label{5-the-faithfulness-boundary}

A verified theorem says one thing: the proof follows from its
specification. Whether that specification denotes the concept a human
meant is a separate question, and the kernel never touches it.
Everything the apparatus guarantees sits on the formal side of that
line; everything a user cares about sits partly on the other side, in
the blueprint, argued in prose. The Shamir result makes the line
concrete.

Our Shamir secret-sharing theorem,
\texttt{shamir\_threshold\_reconstruction\_secrecy\_tightness}, is
kernel-clean and true. It proves three things over an arbitrary field:
any \texttt{t} shares reconstruct the secret; for any \texttt{t−1}
observed shares there is exactly one degree-\texttt{\textless{}t}
polynomial consistent with each candidate secret, so secrets and
consistent polynomials are in bijection; and at the \texttt{t−1}
boundary two distinct secrets each remain possible, so the bound is
tight. The middle leg is named \texttt{PerfectSecrecy} in the file, and
it states existence and uniqueness in full, an \texttt{∃!}, not a
cardinality proxy.

Now the caveat. \texttt{PerfectSecrecy} proves the \emph{algebraic}
secrecy bijection. It does not prove the \emph{measure-theoretic}
Shannon statement, that the posterior probability of the secret given
\texttt{t−1} shares equals its prior. The bijection is the capacity for
perfect secrecy; the probabilistic conclusion needs one more thing the
development deliberately omits, uniform sampling of the
polynomial\textquotesingle s coefficients, which requires a finite field
and a probability space and the measure theory that comes with it. The
file uses \texttt{{[}Field\ F{]}}, correct for the bijection,
insufficient for the sampling step. Separately, the reconstruction
guarantee assumes the submitted shares are the dealer\textquotesingle s
own, so it models basic honest-but-curious Shamir rather than verifiable
secret sharing, where a forged share is the whole threat.

Read carelessly, the symbol \texttt{PerfectSecrecy} over-claims. It
reads like the full cryptographic property while it proves the algebraic
core. A reader might call this laundering, a proof dressed up as more
than it is. That reading misses the boundary. The gap traces entirely to
a scoping choice in the blueprint. The blueprint asked for the algebraic
bijection, so the apparatus proved the algebraic bijection, and the
firewall correctly certified that the Lean statement means what the
blueprint said. The kernel did its job. The apparatus did its job. The
scope was set by the human who wrote the blueprint, and the
file\textquotesingle s own scope caveat says so in plain language.

The system enforces one discipline around this. The blueprint is the
single highest-leverage and highest-risk input in the pipeline. A
faithful formalization of a mis-scoped or over-named blueprint yields a
true theorem that a reader can over-read. No governance check catches
it, because nothing is formally wrong to catch: the proof is valid, the
statement is faithful to the blueprint, the axioms are clean. The error
lives in the denotation, in whether \texttt{PerfectSecrecy} should mean
the algebraic bijection or the Shannon statement, and denotation is
argued rather than proved. The apparatus surfaces this frontier rather
than hiding it. It runs a denotation pass that reports whether a built
definition is pinned by a verified external anchor or merely
self-consistent, and when a definition is only self-consistent it says
so, as a flagged gap, never as a certification. The filed Byzantine
result carries exactly such a flag on one auxiliary definition,
disclosed in its README.

A simple rule falls out. Trust the kernel for what it certifies, that
the proof follows from the specification. Read the blueprint and the
scope caveat for what the specification means. A verified theorem is a
conditional promise: \emph{if} the specification denotes what you think
it does, \emph{then} the property holds, mechanically and beyond doubt.
The apparatus makes the "then" free. It cannot make the "if" free.

\subsection{6. Applications}\label{6-applications}

The apparatus points at several domains. We present four as illustrative
options and one as the frontier, and we rank them by how well the
current machinery fits, because pretending they are equally ready would
repeat the over-claim the faithfulness boundary warns against.

\textbf{Operating-covenant verification.} A credit agreement carries
covenants: leverage ratios, coverage tests, restricted-payment baskets.
Whether a proposed transaction breaches a covenant is a question with a
definite answer, and the waterfall results in the library are a first
step into exactly this capital-structure logic. Formalizing the covenant
faithfully takes the effort; the proof that follows is short.

\textbf{IAM and access-policy state transitions.} "Can a terminated
contractor read production after this role change?" is a decidable
question over linear and boolean constraints, which is where SMT
quantifier elimination is strongest. This is the most decidable of the
four, and it is also the most occupied. AWS Zelkova already does
access-policy verification at production scale. The opening, if there is
one, is in multi-step transition logic and in being general-purpose, not
in out-competing a tool that runs millions of times a day on the static
case.

\textbf{Supply-chain and ledger integrity.} Conservation invariants, no
double-spend, reconciliation across a distributed ledger, these are the
same shape as the AMM invariants, quantities that must be preserved
across an adversarial sequence of operations.

\textbf{Option-value and pricing invariants.} No-arbitrage bounds,
monotonicity of value in the underlying, and put-call parity are
theorems with clean statements the prover can already reach, starting
from the filed FTAP result.

\textbf{A contested-spec covenant, demonstrated.} The most telling case
is not the most decidable one but the one where the proof is easy and
the faithfulness is hard. We ran one: a restricted-payments covenant
from a live SEC EDGAR credit agreement. From a natural-language
instruction the engine abstracted the accounting into opaque givens,
proved the covenant\textquotesingle s two safety invariants (a
continuing default blocks every payment; an exhausted builder-basket
blocks any further one), and a companion faithfulness certificate
refutes each plausible misreading --- \texttt{∧→∨}, a dropped basket
cap, \texttt{\textless{}}→\texttt{≤} --- with a concrete transaction it
would wave through. Abstracting the accounting is a deliberate cut, not
a dodge: the covenant failures that strip creditors are compositional
--- the basket-and-carve-out interlocks a liability-management loop
exploits, not a mis-added balance --- so proving the permission logic
targets the failure mode that matters and keeps the state space
searchable. Two limits remain: the abstraction level is the
human\textquotesingle s scoping choice, not the full financial model;
and a thousand-page agreement at full fidelity is out of reach --- that
gap is the state of the art, per the limitations below.

\subsection{7. Limitations}\label{7-limitations}

\textbf{Autoformalizer at scale.} Turning a hand-written blueprint into
a faithful Lean statement works well for a single theorem. Turning an
unstructured thousand-page regulation or a full production contract into
faithful statements at that volume is the least-validated of the four
stages, and it is what stands between the apparatus and a production
deployment. The proofs themselves are past toy, the filed results are
multi-lemma campaigns, but each was driven from a blueprint a human
wrote. The distance the apparatus covers for one theorem it has not yet
covered for a full document.

\textbf{Denotation.} The guarantee stops at the formal boundary. Whether
a specification denotes the intended concept is argued, not proved, and
no amount of proving capability changes that. It is a permanent property
of what formal verification is, not a bug to be fixed, and the
responsible move is to surface it rather than paper over it.

\textbf{Clean versus messy specifications.} The apparatus is strongest
where the specification is clean: an AMM invariant, a quorum bound, a
pricing theorem. It is weakest where the specification is contested,
ambiguous, or buried in prose, which is exactly where the highest-value
business logic lives. The contested-spec covenant we proposed as the
next frontier expresses this limit directly. Some deterministic search
moves are also redundant on easy targets, where a native tactic already
closes the goal; the specialized levers earn their keep only where the
plain cascade is blind.

\textbf{The search/governance decoupling.} The proof search delegates
entirely to exogenous frontier models: Codex-class and Claude agents
propose every move, and a shell-equipped model could reproduce those
moves itself. The edge is not there, and not in any proprietary weights.
It is in the deterministic governance kernel and the faithfulness
firewall, the layers that re-check every closure and gate every
statement before a proof is attempted, which no model does for itself. A
faster prover slots in behind them, and the guarantee holds regardless
of which model is driving.

\subsection{8. Conclusion}\label{8-conclusion}

A machine-checked proof used to cost six months of a scarce
specialist\textquotesingle s time, so the world verified almost none of
its high-value institutional logic. An autonomous, general-purpose
apparatus drops that cost toward the cost of compute, and
\texttt{nonmathlib4} is the first accumulation of what falls out:
kernel-checked theorems in finance, strategy, distributed systems, and
cryptography, each self-contained, each axiom-clean, each produced from
a natural-language blueprint with no verification engineer in the loop.
The theorems compound, a later one citing an earlier one, so the
marginal cost of the next verified property keeps falling as the library
grows.

A faster prover does not carry the work, and neither does a first
verification of business logic, which already exists and in places is
commercial. The closed loop does: the Lean compiler as an adversarial
ground truth that forces a fallible model to discard its hallucinations,
a governance kernel the model cannot influence, certificates a skeptic
can re-run. So does the discipline of saying where the guarantee stops.
The kernel certifies that the proof follows from the specification; the
blueprint, argued in prose, carries the specification\textquotesingle s
meaning, and the Shamir case shows what happens at that seam. Owning the
seam separates a certificate from a claim.

The next demonstration is one of scale. A contested-spec covenant is
already filed: a restricted-payments clause from a live SEC EDGAR
agreement, its accounting abstracted by a human, its faithfulness argued
in a companion certificate. What the apparatus does not yet do is read
the agreement itself. Today a human draws the abstraction boundary and
hands the engine a scoped blueprint; the thousand-page credit agreement,
with the machine deciding for itself which clauses to formalize, is the
stage it does not yet exercise at scale, and the edge of what it can do
today. A second direction matters as much for adoption: a theorem
library is hard to use, so a navigable surface over the filed results,
one that lets a researcher see which mechanism cites which and where a
constraint would break a baseline, turns the library from an archive
into a workbench. Neither is filed yet. Both are the work in front of
us.

The name states the scope. Mathlib is the library of mathematics.
\texttt{nonmathlib4} is the library of everything else that turns out to
be provable: the mechanisms and institutional logic that stayed
unformalized because the people who could verify it and the people who
understood it were rarely the same, and that a machine can now formalize
from a written blueprint.
